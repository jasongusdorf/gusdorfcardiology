Palpitations and syncope

Correlate symptoms with rhythm and identify the patient who cannot safely wait.

Mechanism

Palpitations may reflect arrhythmia, heightened awareness of normal rhythm, or noncardiac adrenergic states. Syncope is transient global cerebral hypoperfusion; mechanism is reflex, orthostatic, cardiac, or rarely cerebrovascular mimic.

I

Anatomy and physiology

Syncope is cerebral hypoperfusion

True syncope is abrupt, brief loss of consciousness with spontaneous recovery from global cerebral hypoperfusion. Reflex, orthostatic, and cardiac mechanisms dominate; seizure and psychogenic events are mimics.

Palpitations are not always arrhythmia

Ectopy, sinus tachycardia, AF, SVT, VT, hyperadrenergic states, anemia, thyroid disease, drugs, and heightened awareness can feel similar. Symptom–rhythm correlation is decisive.

Cardiac risk clues

Exertion or supine onset, structural disease, abnormal ECG, family sudden death, injury without prodrome, chest pain, dyspnea, or palpitations immediately before syncope raise concern.

II

Clinical reasoning

Reconstruct the event

Interview witnesses; define posture, trigger, prodrome, duration, movements, color, pulse if known, recovery, medications, and prior episodes. Convulsive movement does not automatically mean seizure.

Measure orthostatics correctly

After supine rest, record pressure and heart rate during standing with symptoms; interpret medication, volume, autonomic, and chronotropic responses.

Match monitor to recurrence

Daily symptoms suit Holter; weekly-to-monthly symptoms suit patch/event monitoring; rare high-risk unexplained syncope may require an implantable recorder.

Worked clinical problem

An older patient with aortic stenosis has abrupt syncope while walking uphill without prodrome.

1

High-risk features

Exertion, structural disease, and absent prodrome suggest cardiac output limitation or arrhythmia.

2

Evaluation

Urgent ECG, telemetry, valve severity and hemodynamic assessment are warranted.

3

Do not reassure

A normal orthostatic measurement does not neutralize the high-risk context.

Synthesis

Syncope risk is defined by mechanism and consequence, not by whether the patient feels well afterward.

III

Retrieval practice

Transient global cerebral hypoperfusion with rapid onset, brief duration, and spontaneous recovery.

Yes.

01

Findings

  • High-risk syncope features include exertion, supine events, structural heart disease, family sudden death, abnormal ECG, injury, chest pain, dyspnea, hypotension, or anemia/bleeding.
  • Characterize abruptness, prodrome, triggers, duration, recovery, pulse regularity, and witness observations.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Choose monitor duration by symptom frequency: telemetry, Holter, patch, event monitor, or implantable loop recorder.
  • Use orthostatic vitals correctly and reserve neurologic testing for presentations that suggest seizure or focal disease.
03

Management

  • Admit or expedite evaluation when a dangerous cardiac cause remains plausible.
  • Treat the documented mechanism rather than the symptom label.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Ordering a 24-hour Holter for monthly symptoms.
  • Calling convulsive movements proof of epilepsy.
  • Missing medication-induced orthostasis or QT prolongation.
