Chest pain: a risk-first approach

Identify time-critical causes, estimate coronary probability, and select a testing pathway that changes care.

Mechanism

Chest pain can arise from myocardial ischemia, aorta, pericardium, pulmonary vasculature, pleura, esophagus, chest wall, and referred structures. Quality alone is weak evidence; tempo, context, associated physiology, ECG, and biomarkers do more work.

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

Heart, aorta, lungs, esophagus, muscles, and anxiety can all cause chest pain. First exclude fast killers, then narrow the rest.

↔

A mental model that sticks

A fire alarm may mean fire or steam; first find dangerous heat.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Present onset, provocation, quality, radiation, associated features, risk, exam, ECG, and serial biomarkers; address alternatives.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Character alone cannot exclude ACS.
  • 2. Name lethal mimics.
  • 3. Use serial evidence.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Identify time-critical threats before refining probability.
  2. 02Use symptom features as likelihood modifiers, not exclusion rules.
  3. 03Choose serial testing and disposition from evolving risk.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Six dangerous mechanisms

ACS, aortic syndrome, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, esophageal rupture, and pericardial tamponade require early consideration; myocarditis and severe valve disease can also be urgent.

Pain is imprecise

Pressure, pleurisy, tearing, reproducibility, radiation, and relief alter probability but rarely rule diagnoses in or out alone. Age, sex, diabetes, and communication change presentation.

Time is diagnostic data

Onset, peak, persistence, recurrence, exertion, respiration, position, meals, and treatment response help distinguish ischemia, inflammation, vascular catastrophe, and noncardiac causes.

II

Clinical reasoning

Stabilize while localizing

Obtain vitals in both arms when relevant, focused vascular/cardiopulmonary exam, prompt ECG, serial biomarkers, and targeted imaging without allowing one pathway to blind the others.

Use pathways in their validated population

Risk scores support rather than replace judgment and should not be used when an obvious high-risk feature already mandates action.

Discharge is a clinical intervention

State the working diagnosis, residual uncertainty, return precautions, medication changes, and follow-up—not merely “troponin negative.”

Worked clinical problem

A hypertensive patient develops abrupt maximal chest pain radiating to the back with a new diastolic murmur.

1

Threat

Acute aortic syndrome with aortic regurgitation must be assumed until evaluated.

2

Avoid anchoring

Troponin or coronary changes may occur if the dissection involves a coronary ostium.

3

Action

Urgent aortic imaging and surgical coordination take priority; anticoagulation for presumed ACS may be harmful.

Synthesis

A lethal mimic can produce ischemic findings. The mechanism-first differential prevents a familiar ACS pathway from becoming dangerous autopilot.

III

Retrieval practice

01Can reproducible pain exclude ACS? +

No.

02What does a normal first troponin prove? +

Only that injury is not detected at that sampling time with that assay.

03Name three immediately dangerous noncoronary causes. +

Aortic syndrome, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, or esophageal rupture.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

Chest pain can arise from myocardial ischemia, aorta, pericardium, pulmonary vasculature, pleura, esophagus, chest wall, and referred structures. Quality alone is weak evidence; tempo, context, associated physiology, ECG, and biomarkers do more work.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

First consider ACS, acute aortic syndrome, PE, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, and esophageal rupture. Ask onset-to-peak, exertional relation, pleuritic/positional features, radiation, associated dyspnea/diaphoresis/syncope, and prior episodes.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Obtain early ECG and repeat it with ongoing symptoms; use validated high-sensitivity troponin pathways. Choose no testing, functional testing, coronary CT, or invasive angiography from risk and the decision at stake.

→
4

Act and reassess

Treat instability and suspected occlusion immediately. Use the final diagnosis to drive prevention; a negative ACS evaluation is not a diagnosis.

By the end, you should be able to

  • 01Explain the underlying mechanism without relying on a memorized label.
  • 02Recognize the highest-yield clinical and physiologic findings.
  • 03Choose a test because of the decision it can change.
  • 04Start appropriate management and name what must be reassessed.

Check your understanding

  1. Mechanism: What variable changes first, and what changes downstream?
  2. Discrimination: Which finding best separates this process from its closest mimic?
  3. Testing: What result would actually change what you do next?
  4. Safety: Which common shortcut creates the greatest risk? Hint: Calling pain “atypical” and stopping reasoning.

Common pitfalls

  • Calling pain “atypical” and stopping reasoning.
  • Using pain reproducibility to exclude ACS.
  • Ordering CT angiography without defining whether coronary, aortic, or pulmonary anatomy is the target.
