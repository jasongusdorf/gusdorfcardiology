Shock: phenotype before pressor

Recognize inadequate perfusion, identify the hemodynamic pattern, and treat cause and physiology together.

Mechanism

Shock is inadequate oxygen delivery or utilization. Cardiac presentations may be cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive, hypovolemic, or mixed. Blood pressure is only one signal; compensatory vasoconstriction can temporarily preserve it despite falling output.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Recognize shock before hypotension becomes profound.
  • •Classify distributive, cardiogenic, hypovolemic, and obstructive physiology.
  • •Use serial response to refine treatment.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Pressure is not flow

MAP reflects cardiac output, vascular resistance, and venous pressure. Vasoconstriction can preserve pressure despite low flow; distributive shock can have high flow with maldistributed oxygen delivery.

Oxygen balance

Delivery depends on cardiac output and arterial oxygen content; consumption and extraction determine venous saturation. Lactate can reflect hypoperfusion, adrenergic acceleration, impaired clearance, or other mechanisms.

Mixed shock is common

Sepsis with cardiomyopathy, MI with vasoplegia, hemorrhage with obstruction, or RV failure with infection produce blended profiles that evolve after treatment.

II

Clinical reasoning

Treat while classifying

Secure oxygenation/ventilation, obtain access, monitor perfusion, control hemorrhage or infection, and use focused ultrasound/hemodynamics without delaying definitive intervention.

Choose the variable to change

Fluid increases stressed volume; vasopressor raises vascular tone and perfusion pressure; inotrope raises contractility; diuresis unloads congestion; reperfusion or relief of obstruction treats cause.

Run reassessment loops

After every intervention recheck pressure, capillary refill, mentation, urine, lactate trajectory, JVP, lungs, echo, and adverse effects. Stop treatments that fail the predicted response.

Worked clinical problem

A patient after large anterior MI has cool skin, confusion, MAP 62, JVP 15 cm, pulmonary edema, and low cardiac output.

1

Phenotype

Cold-wet cardiogenic shock with high filling pressure.

2

Avoid reflex fluid

Additional volume is unlikely to improve output and may worsen pulmonary edema.

3

Action

Urgent revascularization/cause control, vasopressor to perfusion pressure, selective inotropy/support, and serial hemodynamic reassessment.

Synthesis

Shock treatment follows the limiting variable. In a congested low-output patient, “low pressure equals fluid” is the wrong equation.

III

Retrieval practice

01Can shock exist with normal blood pressure? +

Yes, if compensatory vasoconstriction preserves MAP despite inadequate flow.

02What determines oxygen delivery? +

Cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content.

03Why is lactate nonspecific? +

It can rise from hypoperfusion, adrenergic glycolysis, impaired clearance, drugs, or mitochondrial dysfunction.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Mean arterial pressure

MAP≈CO×SVR+RAP

Pressure can be restored by flow, resistance, or both; the same MAP can hide very different shock physiology.

Worked example

Given: BP 90/60 mmHg

Substitute: 60 + (90 − 60)/3

Result: 70 mmHg

Decision: The approximation is less reliable at extreme heart rates; adequate MAP does not prove adequate flow.

Shock index

SI=SBPHR​

A rapid screening ratio; trends and clinical context are more useful than a single universal threshold.

Worked example

Given: HR 120/min; SBP 90 mmHg

Substitute: 120 / 90

Result: 1.33

Decision: An elevated or rising value flags risk but does not classify shock or replace organ-perfusion assessment.

01

Findings

  • Look for altered mentation, cool or mottled skin, oliguria, lactate rise, narrow pulse pressure, venous congestion, and respiratory distress.
  • Use history, examination, ECG, POCUS, and response to small interventions to phenotype LV, RV, obstructive, valvular, and distributive components.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Trend perfusion markers and use invasive hemodynamics when the phenotype remains uncertain or the patient fails initial therapy.
  • Search rapidly for MI, mechanical complication, PE, tamponade, dissection, arrhythmia, myocarditis, and sepsis.
03

Management

  • Restore perfusion pressure while arranging definitive treatment.
  • Select vasoactive therapy by the dominant deficit and reassess frequently; mechanical support is a bridge with device-specific harms, not a diagnosis-independent rescue.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Giving repeated fluid boluses without a response target.
  • Using EF alone to define cardiogenic shock.
  • Delaying revascularization or source control while escalating pressors.
