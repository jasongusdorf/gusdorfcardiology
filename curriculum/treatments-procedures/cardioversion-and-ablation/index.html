Cardioversion and ablation

Choose an electrical strategy with a clear mechanism, anticoagulation plan, and definition of success.

Mechanism

Cardioversion terminates an active rhythm but does not remove its substrate. Catheter ablation targets a trigger, critical isthmus, accessory pathway, focus, or arrhythmogenic tissue; effectiveness and procedural risk depend on the mechanism and structural disease.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Choose synchronized versus unsynchronized energy safely.
  2. 02Address sedation and thromboembolism.
  3. 03Explain ablation targets from arrhythmia mechanism.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Energy terminates circuits

Cardioversion depolarizes enough myocardium simultaneously to interrupt reentry and allow the sinus node to resume control. Synchronization avoids delivering a shock on vulnerable ventricular repolarization.

Ablation is mechanism-specific

AVNRT slow pathway, accessory pathway, cavotricuspid isthmus, pulmonary-vein triggers, focal atrial tachycardia, and ventricular scar require different mapping and lesions.

Recurrence is biology

Ablation does not erase hypertension, obesity, sleep apnea, atrial enlargement, ischemia, or cardiomyopathy. Substrate treatment and follow-up remain essential.

II

Clinical reasoning

Stability determines sequence

Unstable tachycardia with a pulse requires synchronized cardioversion; pulseless VT/VF requires unsynchronized defibrillation. Do not delay for perfect rhythm naming.

Plan the whole procedure

Sedation, airway, pad position, anticoagulation, device presence, energy escalation, immediate recurrence, and post-conversion bradycardia must be anticipated.

Consent with real endpoints

Discuss symptom reduction, recurrence, repeat procedures, medication changes, anticoagulation, vascular injury, tamponade, AV block, stroke, and rare mortality according to procedure.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with pre-excited AF has an irregular wide-complex rhythm at 220/min and becomes hypotensive.

1

Danger

Rapid accessory-pathway conduction can degenerate into VF.

2

Avoid

AV-nodal blockers may preferentially direct conduction down the accessory pathway.

3

Action

Perform immediate synchronized cardioversion because the patient is unstable.

Synthesis

The wide irregular rhythm and instability determine safe action. Generic “AF rate control” would be dangerous in this mechanism.

III

Retrieval practice

01Why synchronize cardioversion? +

To avoid shock delivery during the vulnerable T wave.

02What rhythm gets unsynchronized shock? +

Pulseless VT or VF; unstable polymorphic VT also cannot be reliably synchronized.

03Does successful AF ablation automatically stop anticoagulation? +

No; anticoagulation follows thromboembolic risk and clinical guidance.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Electrical cardioversion+

Mechanism

A synchronized shock depolarizes enough myocardium to interrupt a tachycardia while avoiding the vulnerable T wave.

Decision architecture

Address instability, sedation/airway, pads, synchronization, energy escalation, anticoagulation, device status, immediate recurrence, and post-conversion bradycardia.

Clinical anchor: Synchronize when a pulse is present and the rhythm permits reliable sensing.

SVT and flutter ablation+

Mechanism

AVNRT, accessory pathways, focal atrial tachycardia, and typical flutter use discrete nodes, pathways, foci, or isthmuses amenable to targeted lesions.

Decision architecture

Define the circuit from ECG and EP maneuvers, balance recurrence against AV block/vascular risk, and confirm bidirectional block or noninducibility as appropriate.

Clinical anchor: The ablation target follows the circuit, not the surface label “SVT.”

Atrial fibrillation ablation+

Mechanism

Pulmonary-vein triggers interact with atrial substrate; isolation reduces trigger entry while additional lesions are phenotype-dependent.

Decision architecture

Select around symptoms, HF, AF duration, atrial disease, prior drugs, preference, and procedural risk; continue risk-based anticoagulation and modify sustaining factors.

Clinical anchor: Ablation treats arrhythmia burden, not the entire atrial cardiomyopathy.

Ventricular tachycardia ablation+

Mechanism

Scar-related VT follows surviving conducting channels; idiopathic VT can arise from focal or triggered mechanisms.

Decision architecture

Stabilize, define substrate with imaging/electrograms, plan epicardial/hemodynamic support when needed, and integrate drugs, ICD programming, revascularization, and HF care.

Clinical anchor: Eliminating one inducible circuit does not erase the underlying cardiomyopathy.

Complications and recurrence+

Mechanism

Vascular injury, tamponade, stroke, esophageal injury, PV stenosis, coronary injury, AV block, phrenic injury, and recurrent substrate have procedure-specific timing.

Decision architecture

Teach explicit red flags and follow-up; investigate post-procedure hypotension, neurologic change, chest pain, fever/dysphagia, dyspnea, or recurrent tachycardia by mechanism.

Clinical anchor: Recurrence in the blanking period and late recurrence require different interpretation.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Electrical energy

E=∫V(t)I(t)dt

Delivered joules depend on the voltage–current waveform over time; pad position and transthoracic impedance affect current delivery.

Worked example

Given: Average voltage 1,000 V; current 20 A; effective pulse 10 ms

Substitute: 1,000 × 20 × 0.010

Result: ≈200 J

Decision: Actual biphasic waveform energy is integrated over time; pad contact and impedance alter delivered current.

  • Stopping anticoagulation solely because ablation appears successful.
  • Sedating an unstable patient before the team is ready to deliver shock.
  • Failing to correct potassium, magnesium, ischemia, or drug triggers.
