Pacemakers, ICDs, and CRT

Match the device to the electrical problem, sudden-death risk, and ventricular substrate.

Mechanism

Pacemakers prevent pathologic bradycardia; ICDs terminate malignant ventricular arrhythmias; CRT resynchronizes selected dyssynchronous ventricles. Each device introduces lead, infection, vascular, programming, and long-term management tradeoffs.

I

Anatomy and physiology

Three distinct devices

A pacemaker prevents pathologic bradycardia; an ICD detects and treats ventricular tachyarrhythmia; CRT coordinates ventricular activation to improve selected dyssynchronous HF. Many systems combine functions.

Lead–tissue interface

Sensing detects intrinsic signals; capture means a paced stimulus depolarizes myocardium; impedance reflects circuit integrity. Thresholds change with lead position, fibrosis, electrolytes, ischemia, and drugs.

Timing cycles

Lower rate, AV delay, upper tracking, refractory periods, mode switching, and rate response determine behavior. “Failure to pace” may be normal inhibition or programming.

II

Clinical reasoning

Start with device and indication

Identify manufacturer/device type, leads, dependency, battery, programmed modes/zones, recent therapies, and the clinical question before interpreting a strip.

Separate malfunction categories

Undersensing, oversensing, failure to capture, failure to output, lead failure, and inappropriate detection have different ECG and interrogation signatures.

Know magnet uncertainty

Magnet response differs by device type, manufacturer, and programming; it often suspends ICD tachytherapy but does not guarantee a specific pacing response.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with an ICD receives repeated shocks while conscious; telemetry shows a narrow irregular tachycardia.

1

Stabilize

Assess rhythm and hemodynamics; shocks may be inappropriate but can still destabilize.

2

Mechanism

AF with rapid conduction may enter a programmed VT/VF detection zone.

3

Action

Apply a magnet only with continuous monitoring and resuscitation capability when appropriate, treat the rhythm, and obtain urgent interrogation and reprogramming.

Synthesis

A shock is a device event, not a diagnosis. Stored electrograms and programming determine whether detection and therapy were appropriate.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Bradycardia pacing+

Mechanism

A pacemaker provides atrial and/or ventricular output when sinus impulse formation or AV conduction cannot maintain safe rate and synchrony.

Decision architecture

Tie symptoms to mechanism, choose chambers and timing, minimize harmful RV pacing when possible, and document dependency and follow-up.

Clinical anchor: A pacing indication depends on symptoms, site of block, reversibility, and prognosis—not heart rate alone.

ICD therapy+

Mechanism

The device classifies ventricular rate/electrograms and delivers antitachycardia pacing or shock; discrimination can fail in fast AF, noise, or lead problems.

Decision architecture

Match primary/secondary prevention indication to expected survival, program zones and delays, minimize inappropriate therapy, and review every shock with stored electrograms.

Clinical anchor: A shock is a device event; the stored rhythm determines whether it was appropriate.

Cardiac resynchronization+

Mechanism

Selected conduction delay causes mechanically inefficient activation; biventricular or conduction-system pacing can restore coordination and reverse remodeling.

Decision architecture

Use QRS morphology/duration, EF, symptoms, rhythm, pacing burden, anatomy, and expected capture; verify effective delivery and response after implantation.

Clinical anchor: An implanted LV lead does not guarantee effective resynchronization.

Troubleshooting+

Mechanism

Failure to output, capture, sense, or classify can arise from programming, battery, lead, tissue threshold, electrolytes, ischemia, or electromagnetic interference.

Decision architecture

Read surface ECG and stored electrograms with thresholds, impedance, trends, imaging, and clinical context; stabilize first when dependency or shocks create immediate risk.

Clinical anchor: Separate sensing from capture before naming a malfunction.

Periprocedural management+

Mechanism

Electrocautery, MRI, surgery, radiation, and magnets interact differently with pacing and tachytherapy systems.

Decision architecture

Identify exact device, dependency, procedure field/equipment, programmed response, monitoring, reprogramming plan, external rescue, and post-procedure restoration.

Clinical anchor: Magnet behavior is device- and programming-specific; verify rather than assume.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Corrected sinus-node recovery time

CSNRT=SNRT−SCL

An electrophysiology measure of sinus-node recovery after pacing.

Units/terms: SCL = baseline sinus cycle length

Worked example

Given: SNRT 1,700 ms; baseline sinus cycle 900 ms

Substitute: 1,700 − 900

Result: CSNRT 800 ms

Decision: Interpret with autonomic state, medications, symptoms, and the broader electrophysiologic evaluation.

01

Findings

  • Define the indication and whether it is symptom relief, bradycardia protection, primary prevention, secondary prevention, or resynchronization.
  • Read paced ECGs for chamber captured, morphology, and failure to sense/capture.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Document EF after adequate therapy and waiting periods when primary-prevention ICD decisions depend on reversibility.
  • Interrogate devices for battery, lead integrity, arrhythmia episodes, therapies, and pacing burden.
03

Management

  • Use shared decision-making that includes competing mortality, likelihood of benefit, shocks, generator changes, and deactivation preferences.
  • Address high RV pacing burden and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Implanting before reversible causes or recovery windows are addressed.
  • Equating detected high-rate episodes with clinical AF without reviewing electrograms.
  • Forgetting device infection when bacteremia has no clear source.
