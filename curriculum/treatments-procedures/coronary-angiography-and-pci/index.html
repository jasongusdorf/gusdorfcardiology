Coronary angiography and PCI

Understand anatomy, physiology, indication, procedural risk, and post-PCI care.

Mechanism

Angiography shows a luminogram; intracoronary imaging shows vessel and plaque structure; pressure-based physiology estimates whether a lesion limits flow under defined conditions. PCI treats focal epicardial obstruction but not the systemic atherosclerotic process.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Interpret angiography as anatomy rather than automatic ischemia.
  • •Use coronary physiology and intravascular imaging appropriately.
  • •Plan PCI around indication, anatomy, bleeding, kidneys, and complications.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Luminography has limits

Angiography outlines contrast-filled lumen in two dimensions. Foreshortening, overlap, diffuse disease, positive remodeling, and visual estimation can hide or exaggerate lesion severity.

Physiology asks a different question

FFR and nonhyperemic pressure ratios estimate whether a lesion limits pressure/flow under defined conditions. They do not measure plaque vulnerability or microvascular mechanism.

PCI changes specific outcomes

In acute occlusion PCI saves myocardium; in chronic disease its symptom and event effects depend on presentation, anatomy, ischemic burden, ventricular function, and comparison therapy.

II

Clinical reasoning

Define the indication first

Culprit ACS, ongoing ischemia, shock anatomy, refractory angina, left main disease, and stable intermediate lesions have different evidence and urgency.

Plan antithrombotics and access

Bleeding risk, radial access, anticoagulants, upcoming surgery, adherence, and stent strategy belong in the pre-procedure plan.

Anticipate failure modes

Dissection, perforation, no-reflow, side-branch loss, thrombosis, contrast-associated injury, bleeding, and stroke require immediate recognition and prevention.

Worked clinical problem

A stable patient has a visually 60% mid-LAD lesion and symptoms not clearly exertional.

1

Do not treat the picture

Visual intermediate stenosis does not establish symptom-causing ischemia.

2

Clarify mechanism

Optimize medical therapy and use validated physiology when revascularization would depend on lesion significance.

3

Broaden if negative

A negative epicardial measure should prompt consideration of microvascular disease, spasm, or noncardiac symptoms—not dismissal.

Synthesis

PCI should answer a clinical question. An angiographic narrowing is anatomy; physiology and presentation determine whether opening it is useful.

Retrieval practice

No.

It generally reduces access-site bleeding and vascular complications.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Access and diagnostic angiography+

Mechanism

Arterial access and contrast projections create a two-dimensional luminogram of three-dimensional coronary anatomy.

Decision architecture

Choose radial/femoral access, anticoagulation, contrast strategy, and projections around anatomy, bleeding, kidneys, prior grafts, and possible support needs.

Clinical anchor: Angiography depicts lumen, not plaque biology or microvascular function.

Coronary physiology+

Mechanism

FFR compares distal and proximal pressure during hyperemia; resting indices sample different flow conditions; CFR and microvascular resistance interrogate additional compartments.

Decision architecture

Use physiology for appropriate intermediate disease, verify drift/damping/hyperemia, and match the measured compartment to the clinical question.

Clinical anchor: A normal epicardial pressure ratio does not exclude microvascular angina or vasospasm.

Lesion preparation and stenting+

Mechanism

Ballooning and stenting enlarge lumen by displacing/compressing plaque and scaffolding dissection; calcification and bifurcation anatomy alter expansion.

Decision architecture

Use intravascular imaging when anatomy/optimization warrants, size to reference vessel, ensure expansion/apposition, preserve side branches, and plan antiplatelet therapy.

Clinical anchor: Underexpansion is a preventable substrate for restenosis and thrombosis.

Acute complications+

Mechanism

Dissection, perforation, no-reflow, acute thrombosis, side-branch loss, embolization, arrhythmia, and hemodynamic collapse have distinct angiographic signatures.

Decision architecture

Recognize the mechanism in real time and deploy the specific remedy—balloon/stent, reversal, covered stent, vasodilator, aspiration, support, or surgery—while stabilizing perfusion.

Clinical anchor: “Chest pain after PCI” is not a diagnosis; define vessel patency, flow, injury, and competing causes.

Aftercare and secondary prevention+

Mechanism

PCI treats a focal lesion while systemic atherosclerosis and thrombosis risk persist.

Decision architecture

Specify antiplatelet duration, access/contrast follow-up, lipid and pressure therapy, smoking cessation, rehabilitation, symptom plan, and what requires urgent reassessment.

Clinical anchor: Successful PCI is the start of secondary prevention, not its substitute.

01

Findings

  • Define indication: acute occlusion, high-risk ACS, refractory symptoms, or prognostically important anatomy.
  • Describe lesion location, complexity, flow, collateralization, and the myocardium at risk.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Use FFR/iFR or intracoronary imaging when visual severity is uncertain and the result will change intervention.
  • Assess renal, bleeding, vascular-access, and antithrombotic risk before the procedure.
03

Management

  • Match stent and antiplatelet strategy to ischemic and bleeding risk.
  • Continue aggressive lipid, lifestyle, rehabilitation, and risk-factor treatment after successful PCI.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Treating the angiogram rather than the patient.
  • Calling intermediate visual stenosis flow-limiting without physiology.
  • Failing to plan DAPT before a procedure that may interrupt it.
