Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Explain cardiovascular drugs through targets and physiologic variables.
  2. 02Separate symptomatic, hemodynamic, and outcome effects.
  3. 03Anticipate kinetics, interactions, contraindications, and monitoring.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Target to phenotype

A drug is appropriate when its molecular effect addresses the patient’s mechanism: reduce thrombosis, volume, afterload, rate, oxygen demand, remodeling, or lipids. Class labels do not replace indication.

Time course matters

IV vasodilators act in minutes; diuretic natriuresis follows delivery to the tubule; remodeling therapies improve outcomes over months. Half-life does not equal duration for irreversible or downstream effects.

Benefit and harm share mechanisms

RAAS blockade lowers pressure and remodeling but can raise potassium/creatinine; beta blockade reduces adrenergic harm but can worsen acute low-output states; antithrombotics prevent clot at the cost of bleeding.

II

Clinical reasoning

Prescribe with a monitoring contract

State indication, starting dose, titration goal, expected effect, hold parameters, renal/hepatic adjustment, interactions, laboratory schedule, and what the patient should report.

Interpret early changes correctly

A creatinine or pressure change may be expected, tolerable, or dangerous depending on magnitude, perfusion, potassium, congestion, and trajectory.

Deprescribe actively

Remove duplicate, nonbeneficial, interacting, or contraindicated therapy when physiology changes; medication lists otherwise accumulate historical decisions.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with HFrEF starts an SGLT2 inhibitor and has a small early eGFR dip without hypotension, volume depletion, or ketoacidosis symptoms.

1

Expected physiology

Reduced intraglomerular pressure can cause a modest initial filtration dip.

2

Safety check

Assess volume, pressure, illness, intake, renal trajectory, and interacting diuretics rather than reflex discontinuation.

3

Long-term frame

Cardiorenal outcome benefit generally outweighs a small stable early dip.

Synthesis

Monitoring distinguishes an expected mechanistic effect from true intolerance. The drug should not be judged by a single isolated laboratory value.

III

Retrieval practice

01Does half-life always equal pharmacodynamic duration? +

No; receptor binding, active metabolites, and downstream biology can extend or shorten effects.

02What must every prescription include? +

Indication, dose/titration, monitoring, interactions, hold rules, and patient counseling.

03Why can beta blockers harm acute cardiogenic shock? +

Negative chronotropy/inotropy may reduce already inadequate output.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Heart-failure disease modification+

Mechanism

RAAS/ARNI, beta-blockade, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibition interrupt distinct maladaptive pathways; diuretics primarily relieve congestion.

Decision architecture

Start foundational classes early at tolerable doses, monitor pressure/renal function/potassium/volume, and distinguish expected changes from true intolerance.

Clinical anchor: Outcome benefit comes from complementary mechanisms, not maximal dosing of one drug while omitting others.

Antithrombotic therapy+

Mechanism

Antiplatelets inhibit platelet activation; anticoagulants suppress thrombin generation. Indication, onset, clearance, and reversal differ.

Decision architecture

Balance thrombosis and bleeding using the actual indication, renal/hepatic function, interacting drugs, planned procedures, adherence, and duration; avoid unnecessary combination therapy.

Clinical anchor: More antithrombotic intensity is not automatically more net benefit.

Rate and rhythm drugs+

Mechanism

Beta blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium blockers, digoxin, and antiarrhythmics alter automaticity, AV conduction, refractoriness, or contractility.

Decision architecture

Choose for rhythm mechanism and substrate; account for structural disease, QT/QRS, organ clearance, interactions, bradycardia, and proarrhythmia; define monitoring before discharge.

Clinical anchor: A drug that terminates a rhythm may be unsafe for maintaining it in a particular substrate.

Anti-ischemic and lipid therapy+

Mechanism

Nitrates and rate/pressure reduction change demand or supply; statins and adjunctive lipid drugs reduce atherogenic exposure and events rather than treating today’s pain.

Decision architecture

Separate symptom control from event prevention, target LDL reduction to risk, address adherence/secondary causes, and escalate therapy when exposure remains above goal.

Clinical anchor: Relief with nitroglycerin is not a diagnostic test for coronary ischemia.

Hypertension and vascular drugs+

Mechanism

Diuretics, RAAS blockade, calcium-channel blockers, sympatholytics, and vasodilators modify volume, resistance, or neurohormonal drive with phenotype-specific tradeoffs.

Decision architecture

Confirm measurement and out-of-office burden, identify emergencies/secondary causes, choose combinations around comorbidity, and monitor orthostasis, electrolytes, renal function, and adherence.

Clinical anchor: A blood-pressure number must be interpreted with organ injury, time course, and measurement quality.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

Cardiovascular drugs change volume, vascular tone, neurohormonal signaling, thrombosis, lipids, rate, rhythm, contractility, or remodeling. A mechanism map prevents memorized lists from becoming disconnected from physiology.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

For every drug, know the target, desired clinical outcome, expected physiologic effect, major contraindications, and toxicity signature. Separate symptom-relieving therapy from therapy proven to alter hospitalization, stroke, MI, or mortality.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Define baseline renal function, electrolytes, blood pressure, rhythm/QT, and interacting drugs as relevant. Set a time for laboratory and clinical reassessment when prescribing.

→
4

Act and reassess

Prioritize therapies with outcome benefit, then titrate in an order the patient can tolerate. Use indication-specific antithrombotic intensity and duration; more therapy is not automatically more protection.

By the end, you should be able to

  • 01Explain the underlying mechanism without relying on a memorized label.
  • 02Recognize the highest-yield clinical and physiologic findings.
  • 03Choose a test because of the decision it can change.
  • 04Start appropriate management and name what must be reassessed.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Drug half-life

t1/2​=CL0.693Vd​​

Volume of distribution and clearance determine the time course; organ dysfunction can therefore change accumulation.

Worked example

Given: Volume of distribution 50 L; clearance 5 L/h

Substitute: 0.693 × 50 / 5

Result: 6.93 hours

Decision: After roughly 4–5 half-lives, most accumulation or elimination is complete if kinetics are linear.

Common pitfalls

  • Stopping RAAS inhibition for small creatinine changes without assessing volume and potassium.
  • Combining QT-prolonging or AV-nodal drugs without monitoring.
  • Leaving a medication list without explicit indications.
