Cardiovascular pharmacology by mechanism

Choose drugs by target, outcome, patient phenotype, monitoring burden, and interaction risk.

Mechanism

Cardiovascular drugs change volume, vascular tone, neurohormonal signaling, thrombosis, lipids, rate, rhythm, contractility, or remodeling. A mechanism map prevents memorized lists from becoming disconnected from physiology.

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

Cardiac drugs change pressure, volume, rate, contraction, clotting, or remodeling. Ask which variable changes, how quickly, and at what cost.

↔

A mental model that sticks

Each drug turns control knobs in a coupled system; one knob moves others.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

For one drug: target, physiologic effect, outcome benefit, time course, monitoring, contraindications, and organ-function adjustment.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Separate symptoms from outcomes.
  • 2. Anticipate interactions.
  • 3. Explain hold rules.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

Cardiovascular drugs change volume, vascular tone, neurohormonal signaling, thrombosis, lipids, rate, rhythm, contractility, or remodeling. A mechanism map prevents memorized lists from becoming disconnected from physiology.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

For every drug, know the target, desired clinical outcome, expected physiologic effect, major contraindications, and toxicity signature. Separate symptom-relieving therapy from therapy proven to alter hospitalization, stroke, MI, or mortality.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Define baseline renal function, electrolytes, blood pressure, rhythm/QT, and interacting drugs as relevant. Set a time for laboratory and clinical reassessment when prescribing.

→
4

Act and reassess

Prioritize therapies with outcome benefit, then titrate in an order the patient can tolerate. Use indication-specific antithrombotic intensity and duration; more therapy is not automatically more protection.

By the end, you should be able to

  • 01Explain the underlying mechanism without relying on a memorized label.
  • 02Recognize the highest-yield clinical and physiologic findings.
  • 03Choose a test because of the decision it can change.
  • 04Start appropriate management and name what must be reassessed.

Check your understanding

  1. Mechanism: What variable changes first, and what changes downstream?
  2. Discrimination: Which finding best separates this process from its closest mimic?
  3. Testing: What result would actually change what you do next?
  4. Safety: Which common shortcut creates the greatest risk? Hint: Stopping RAAS inhibition for small creatinine changes without assessing volume and potassium.

Findings

Tests and interpretation

  • Define baseline renal function, electrolytes, blood pressure, rhythm/QT, and interacting drugs as relevant.
  • Set a time for laboratory and clinical reassessment when prescribing.
03

Management

  • Prioritize therapies with outcome benefit, then titrate in an order the patient can tolerate.
  • Use indication-specific antithrombotic intensity and duration; more therapy is not automatically more protection.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Stopping RAAS inhibition for small creatinine changes without assessing volume and potassium.
  • Combining QT-prolonging or AV-nodal drugs without monitoring.
  • Leaving a medication list without explicit indications.
