Foundations • Core

Cardiac anatomy and flow

Trace blood, pressure, and electrical activation through chambers, valves, coronary beds, and the conduction system.

Mechanism

The right heart is a low-pressure, preload-sensitive circuit in series with a high-pressure left heart. Valves open and close because of pressure gradients, not because they actively move. Coronary perfusion is predominantly diastolic in the left ventricle, which explains why tachycardia and low diastolic pressure threaten subendocardial flow.

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

The heart is two pumps side by side. The right pump sends used blood to the lungs; the left sends oxygen-rich blood to the body. Four one-way doors keep it moving forward.

↔

A mental model that sticks

Picture two houses in series, each with a receiving room, a pumping room, and one-way doors. A blockage backs traffic up; a leaky door makes the pump move the same blood twice.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Starting in the vena cava, narrate one complete trip to the aorta. At every valve, state what pressure opens it and what backs up if it narrows or leaks.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Name chambers and valves in order.
  • 2. Explain why left-coronary flow peaks in diastole.
  • 3. Predict right- versus left-sided congestion.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

The right heart is a low-pressure, preload-sensitive circuit in series with a high-pressure left heart. Valves open and close because of pressure gradients, not because they actively move. Coronary perfusion is predominantly diastolic in the left ventricle, which explains why tachycardia and low diastolic pressure threaten subendocardial flow.

2

Recognize the pattern

Localize murmurs by the valve and direction of turbulent flow. Use venous congestion to infer right-sided filling pressure and pulmonary findings to infer left-sided pressure. Map coronary territories before interpreting ischemic ECG or wall-motion findings.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Orient every echocardiographic view by chamber and valve rather than memorized screen position. Treat invasive pressure tracings as simultaneous anatomic and physiologic maps.

→
4

Act and reassess

Ask which chamber, valve, vessel, or conduction structure is failing before naming a syndrome. Predict the upstream congestion and downstream hypoperfusion created by each lesion.

Check your understanding

  1. Mechanism: What variable changes first, and what changes downstream?
  2. Discrimination: Which finding best separates this process from its closest mimic?
  3. Testing: What result would actually change what you do next?
  4. Safety: Which common shortcut creates the greatest risk? Hint: Equating a loud murmur with severe disease.
