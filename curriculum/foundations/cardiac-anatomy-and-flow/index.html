Cardiac anatomy and flow

Trace blood, pressure, and electrical activation through chambers, valves, coronary beds, and the conduction system.

Mechanism

The right heart is a low-pressure, preload-sensitive circuit in series with a high-pressure left heart. Valves open and close because of pressure gradients, not because they actively move. Coronary perfusion is predominantly diastolic in the left ventricle, which explains why tachycardia and low diastolic pressure threaten subendocardial flow.

Three layers of understanding

5

Explain it like I’m five

The heart is two pumps side by side. The right pump sends used blood to the lungs; the left sends oxygen-rich blood to the body. Four one-way doors keep it moving forward.

↔

A mental model that sticks

Picture two houses in series, each with a receiving room, a pumping room, and one-way doors. A blockage backs traffic up; a leaky door makes the pump move the same blood twice.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Starting in the vena cava, narrate one complete trip to the aorta. At every valve, state what pressure opens it and what backs up if it narrows or leaks.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Name chambers and valves in order.
  • 2. Explain why left-coronary flow peaks in diastole.
  • 3. Predict right- versus left-sided congestion.

I

Anatomy and physiology

Position and surfaces

The heart lies obliquely behind the sternum. The right ventricle forms most of the anterior surface; the left atrium is predominantly posterior; the left ventricle forms the apex and left border; the right atrium forms the right border. This geometry explains why parasternal imaging favors the RV and why left-atrial enlargement can affect the esophagus.

Four chambers, unequal jobs

Thin-walled atria are reservoirs, conduits, and booster pumps. The crescentic RV wraps around the LV and tolerates volume better than abrupt pressure. The conical LV supplies systemic pressure. The interventricular septum is shared machinery: RV pressure or volume loading can flatten it and impair LV filling.

Valves and apparatus

The tricuspid and mitral valves depend on annulus, leaflets, chordae, papillary muscles, and ventricular geometry. Papillary muscles prevent prolapse; they do not open the valve. Aortic and pulmonic valves are semilunar valves without chordae. Valve motion is passive and follows pressure reversal.

Coronary and conduction anatomy

The left main divides into LAD and circumflex; the RCA usually supplies the inferior wall and AV node. Dominance is defined by the artery giving rise to the posterior descending artery. The SA node initiates activation, the AV node delays it, and the His–Purkinje network rapidly recruits both ventricles.

II

Clinical reasoning

Localize before labeling

Ask which structure is abnormal, what direction blood or activation should travel, and what lies upstream and downstream. A lesion at the mitral valve raises left-atrial and pulmonary pressure; a lesion at the RV outflow tract does not.

Use three coordinate systems

Surface anatomy predicts auscultation and access; tomographic anatomy predicts echo, CT, and MRI planes; vascular anatomy predicts ECG and wall-motion territories. Keep these maps aligned rather than memorizing isolated lists.

Translate anatomy into urgency

Acute papillary-muscle rupture can cause severe MR with a deceptively soft murmur; proximal LAD occlusion threatens a large LV territory; AV-node ischemia can produce bradycardia. The same named structure must be linked to time course and physiology.

Worked clinical problem

A patient develops hypotension and pulmonary edema three days after an inferior myocardial infarction. A new apical systolic murmur is present but not loud.

1

Structure

Inferior infarction can injure the posteromedial papillary muscle, which usually has a single coronary supply.

2

Flow

Loss of mitral support produces acute systolic flow from LV to LA, abruptly raising pulmonary venous pressure.

3

Why the quiet murmur?

Rapid LV–LA pressure equalization and low forward output may limit intensity despite catastrophic regurgitation.

4

Next action

Obtain urgent echocardiography while stabilizing afterload and perfusion and mobilizing surgical expertise.

Synthesis

The anatomical diagnosis is acute ischemic mitral apparatus failure, not merely “post-MI heart failure.” Anatomy determines the mechanism, the misleading examination, and the need for immediate imaging and intervention.

III

Retrieval practice

01Which chamber is most anterior? +

The right ventricle.

02What defines coronary dominance? +

The artery that gives rise to the posterior descending artery.

03Why is LV coronary perfusion predominantly diastolic? +

Systolic LV pressure compresses intramyocardial vessels, especially in the subendocardium.

04Do papillary muscles open the AV valves? +

No. Pressure gradients open valves; papillary muscles restrain prolapse during systole.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Cardiac output

CO=HR×SV

Flow per minute is heart rate multiplied by blood ejected per beat.

Units/terms: CO L/min; HR beats/min; SV L/beat

Worked example

Given: HR 80/min; stroke volume 70 mL

Substitute: 80 × 0.070 L

Result: 5.6 L/min

Decision: A normal output does not exclude high filling pressure; pair flow with congestion and perfusion.

Oxygen delivery

DO2​=CO×CaO2​×10

Systemic oxygen delivery couples pump flow to arterial oxygen content.

Units/terms: DO₂ mL/min; CO L/min; CaO₂ mL/dL

Worked example

Given: CO 5 L/min; CaO₂ 18 mL/dL

Substitute: 5 × 18 × 10

Result: 900 mL O₂/min

Decision: A patient can have an adequate output yet impaired delivery from severe anemia or hypoxemia.

01

Findings

  • Localize murmurs by the valve and direction of turbulent flow.
  • Use venous congestion to infer right-sided filling pressure and pulmonary findings to infer left-sided pressure.
  • Map coronary territories before interpreting ischemic ECG or wall-motion findings.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Orient every echocardiographic view by chamber and valve rather than memorized screen position.
  • Treat invasive pressure tracings as simultaneous anatomic and physiologic maps.
03

Management

  • Ask which chamber, valve, vessel, or conduction structure is failing before naming a syndrome.
  • Predict the upstream congestion and downstream hypoperfusion created by each lesion.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Equating a loud murmur with severe disease.
  • Forgetting that acute severe regurgitation can be soft because pressures equalize rapidly.
