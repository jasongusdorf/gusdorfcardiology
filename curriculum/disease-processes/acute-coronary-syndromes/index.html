Disease processes • Core

Acute coronary syndromes

Recognize acute coronary occlusion, estimate ischemic and bleeding risk, and coordinate reperfusion and secondary prevention.

Mechanism

Most ACS follows disruption or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque with thrombosis, but coronary spasm, dissection, embolism, microvascular dysfunction, and supply-demand imbalance require different treatment.

0

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

A plaque can rupture and clot, suddenly starving myocardium. Recognize threatened muscle, restore flow, and avoid treating every injury as thrombosis.

A mental model that sticks

A highway narrows for years, then debris abruptly blocks it; opening the road saves the neighborhood downstream.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Move from symptoms through serial ECG/troponin to reperfusion, then contrast plaque rupture with demand injury, spasm, embolism, and dissection.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Recognize nonclassic occlusion patterns.
  • 2. Do not delay clear reperfusion.
  • 3. Balance ischemia and bleeding.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Recognize acute coronary occlusion beyond memorized STEMI criteria.
  • •Separate type 1 MI from other injury mechanisms.
  • •Balance reperfusion, antithrombotic benefit, and bleeding risk.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Plaque and thrombus

Plaque rupture or erosion exposes thrombogenic material, causing platelet activation and thrombin generation. Occlusion may be complete, intermittent, distal, or rapidly reperfused, so ECG and biomarkers evolve.

Ischemic cascade

Perfusion abnormality precedes diastolic dysfunction, systolic dysfunction, ECG change, and pain. Duration and collateral flow determine necrosis. Troponin rise may lag a treatable occlusion.

Territory and complications

The culprit location predicts myocardium at risk, reciprocal changes, conduction disturbance, RV involvement, papillary failure, septal rupture, and shock.

II

Clinical reasoning

Serial, parallel diagnosis

Obtain ECG within minutes, repeat during symptoms, compare prior tracings, measure serial troponin, and evaluate aorta, PE, pericardium, GI, and chest-wall alternatives without delaying reperfusion.

Reperfuse threatened myocardium

Diagnostic occlusion patterns and instability demand emergent angiography even when classic millimeter criteria are absent. Use fibrinolysis only in appropriate STEMI settings when timely PCI is unavailable.

Individualize adjuncts

Antiplatelet, anticoagulant, statin, beta blocker, RAAS therapy, and revascularization choices depend on anatomy, hemodynamics, bleeding, kidneys, and comorbidity.

Worked clinical problem

A patient has crushing pain, ST depression in V1–V3, and tall anterior R waves.

1

Pattern

Anterior reciprocal changes suggest posterior transmural injury.

2

Confirmation without delay

Record posterior leads V7–V9 and activate the reperfusion pathway if clinically coherent.

3

Anatomy

Circumflex or RCA territory may be occluded despite no standard-lead ST elevation.

Synthesis

The standard 12 leads do not directly face the posterior wall. Reciprocal reasoning prevents a false “NSTEMI” delay in an occlusion MI.

Retrieval practice

Yes; biomarkers may lag ischemia.

Posterior infarction/ischemia as reciprocal change.

03Does every type 2 MI need DAPT? +

No; treat the mismatch and individualize coronary evaluation and antithrombotic therapy.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Fractional flow reserve

FFR=Pa​Pd​​ during maximal hyperemia

A pressure ratio used to estimate whether an epicardial stenosis limits maximal flow; it is not a plaque-vulnerability test.

Worked example

Given: Distal pressure 72; aortic pressure 90 mmHg during hyperemia

Substitute: 72 / 90

Result: FFR 0.80

Decision: Interpret the borderline value with symptoms, technical quality, diffuse disease, and the revascularization decision.

01

Findings

  • Use symptoms, serial ECGs, serial high-sensitivity troponin, bedside physiology, and alternative diagnoses together.
  • STEMI-equivalent occlusion patterns can exist without classic millimeter criteria.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Do not delay reperfusion for biomarkers when the ECG and presentation establish an acute occlusion syndrome.
  • Use early angiography according to instability and ischemic risk in NSTE-ACS.
03

Management

  • Give antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy according to the ACS/reperfusion strategy and bleeding risk.
  • Revascularize the culprit urgently when indicated; plan complete revascularization in the appropriate setting.
  • Start intensive lipid lowering, smoking treatment, rehabilitation, and disease-modifying secondary prevention.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Conflating type 2 MI with plaque rupture ACS.
  • Missing posterior, right-ventricular, or high-lateral occlusion.
  • Continuing triple antithrombotic therapy longer than the net-benefit window.
