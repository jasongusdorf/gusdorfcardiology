Disease processes • Core

Valvular heart disease

Integrate valve anatomy, lesion severity, ventricular response, symptoms, and timing of intervention.

Mechanism

Stenosis creates a pressure load; regurgitation creates a volume load. Chronic compensation can preserve symptoms and EF until myocardial reserve is lost, while acute lesions can cause shock or pulmonary edema before dilation develops.

Three layers of understanding

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Connect each valve lesion to chamber loading and compensation.
  • •Integrate morphology, flow, quantitative severity, and consequences.
  • •Recognize intervention triggers before irreversible damage.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Pressure versus volume load

Stenosis creates an upstream pressure load; regurgitation creates a volume load shared by upstream and downstream chambers. Compensation preserves symptoms and EF until reserve is exhausted.

Severity is flow-dependent

Velocity and gradient rise with flow; low flow can hide severe stenosis. Regurgitant measures change with loading, rhythm, jet geometry, and measurement quality.

Anatomy determines options

Leaflet morphology, annular size, subvalvular apparatus, calcification, aorta, ventricular geometry, surgical risk, and durability shape repair, replacement, or transcatheter strategy.

II

Clinical reasoning

Use an integrative grade

Do not let one discordant number dominate. Recheck acquisition and integrate valve area, gradient, flow, chamber response, pulmonary pressure, symptoms, and exercise data.

Separate symptoms from attribution

Dyspnea in an older patient may reflect valve disease, HFpEF, lung disease, anemia, or deconditioning. Exercise testing can expose objective limitation.

Refer before end-stage consequence

Symptoms, LV dysfunction/dilation, pulmonary hypertension, new AF, aortic enlargement, or very severe disease may trigger intervention depending on lesion and guideline.

Worked clinical problem

An asymptomatic patient has AVA 0.8 cm², mean gradient 28 mmHg, EF 35%, and low stroke-volume index.

1

Discordance

Valve area suggests severe AS while gradient is low because transvalvular flow is low.

2

Clarification

Verify LVOT/Doppler measurements and use low-dose dobutamine or CT calcium scoring as appropriate.

3

Decision

Distinguish true severe from pseudo-severe stenosis before intervention.

Synthesis

A low gradient does not exclude severe stenosis when the ventricle cannot generate flow. Resolve the flow–severity discordance rather than averaging labels.

III

Retrieval practice

01Why is EF misleading in chronic MR? +

Total EF includes blood ejected backward into the low-pressure left atrium.

02What measurement error strongly affects AVA? +

LVOT diameter, because area depends on diameter squared.

03Can a severe lesion have a quiet murmur? +

Yes, especially with low flow or acute pressure equalization.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Aortic stenosis+

Mechanism

Fixed LV outflow obstruction creates pressure overload, concentric remodeling, impaired coronary reserve, and eventually low flow or failure.

Decision architecture

Integrate morphology, Vmax, mean gradient, valve area, stroke-volume index, symptoms, EF, exercise response, and CT calcium when discordant; refer before irreversible LV injury.

Clinical anchor: Low gradient does not exclude severe AS when flow is low.

Aortic regurgitation+

Mechanism

Diastolic aortic runoff volume-loads the LV; chronic eccentric remodeling preserves output until reserve fails, while acute AR rapidly raises LV diastolic pressure.

Decision architecture

Define mechanism, vena contracta/flow reversal/regurgitant burden, LV dimensions and EF; acute severe AR is a surgical emergency and chronic intervention precedes end-stage dilation.

Clinical anchor: A normal-looking EF can already represent impaired contractile reserve in severe AR.

Mitral stenosis+

Mechanism

Obstructed diastolic LA-to-LV flow raises LA and pulmonary pressure, especially with tachycardia, exercise, pregnancy, or loss of atrial contraction.

Decision architecture

Use valve morphology, planimetry, gradient at stated heart rate, pulmonary pressure, symptoms, and exercise data to select surveillance, balloon commissurotomy, or surgery.

Clinical anchor: Gradient is flow and rate dependent; valve area and consequences complete the grade.

Primary and secondary MR+

Mechanism

Primary MR begins in the valve apparatus; secondary MR arises from ventricular/atrial geometry. Both volume-load LA and LV, but intervention evidence and targets differ.

Decision architecture

Quantify with an integrative method, define mechanism and repairability, optimize ventricular therapy for secondary MR, and intervene according to symptoms, LV response, pulmonary pressure, AF, and anatomy.

Clinical anchor: Total EF overstates forward performance because regurgitant volume ejects into a low-pressure atrium.

Tricuspid regurgitation+

Mechanism

Most TR is secondary to annular dilation, RV remodeling, pulmonary pressure, AF, or a transvalvular lead; systemic venous congestion drives organ injury.

Decision architecture

Define lead/leaflet mechanism, RV size/function, pulmonary hemodynamics, and end-organ effects; optimize cause and volume while referring before advanced RV or hepatic failure.

Clinical anchor: TR severity and RV failure can be underestimated when right-sided pressure and flow fall.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Aortic valve area

AVA=VTIAV​CSALVOT​×VTILVOT​​

The continuity equation is vulnerable to LVOT diameter error, irregular rhythm, and poor Doppler alignment.

Worked example

Given: LVOT area 3.0 cm²; LVOT VTI 18 cm; AV VTI 72 cm

Substitute: 3.0 × 18 / 72

Result: 0.75 cm²

Decision: Resolve discordance using flow, gradients, measurement review, and adjunctive testing.

Mitral valve area by pressure half-time

MVA≈PHT220​

Useful in selected mitral stenosis; unreliable when chamber compliance or flow changes materially.

Worked example

Given: Pressure half-time 180 ms

Substitute: 220 / 180

Result: MVA ≈ 1.22 cm²

Decision: Avoid this shortcut after valvotomy or when compliance and flow make the constant unreliable.

01

Findings

  • Define valve, lesion, mechanism, severity, chamber consequence, pulmonary pressure, symptoms, and trajectory.
  • Discordance between examination, Doppler measures, chamber remodeling, and symptoms should trigger technical review and multimodality assessment.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Comprehensive TTE is first-line; TEE clarifies anatomy, CT defines calcification and procedural anatomy, and stress testing unmasks symptoms in selected apparently asymptomatic disease.
  • Use dimensionless and flow-independent measures when gradients are flow-dependent or discordant.
03

Management

  • Intervene for symptoms or guideline-defined ventricular consequences when the lesion is severe and treatment benefit exceeds procedural risk.
  • Choose surgery versus transcatheter therapy through anatomy, age/longevity, comorbidity, patient preference, and Heart Team review.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Using EF 50% as “normal” in chronic MR.
  • Calling low-gradient AS moderate without resolving flow state and measurement error.
  • Delaying intervention until irreversible ventricular or pulmonary vascular damage.
