Cardiomyopathies

Use phenotype, family history, imaging, rhythm, and systemic clues to identify myocardial disease.

Mechanism

Cardiomyopathy is myocardial disease not explained solely by loading conditions or coronary disease. Hypertrophic, dilated, arrhythmogenic, restrictive, and nondilated phenotypes are starting points; genetic, infiltrative, inflammatory, toxic, metabolic, and tachycardia-mediated causes cross those boundaries.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Classify phenotype without mistaking it for etiology.
  2. 02Use pedigree, CMR, labs, genetics, and biopsy selectively.
  3. 03Translate diagnosis into family and sudden-death management.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Phenotype is an entry point

Dilated, hypertrophic, restrictive, arrhythmogenic, and noncompaction patterns overlap. Ischemia, valve disease, loading, athletic remodeling, and systemic disease can mimic primary cardiomyopathy.

Tissue and genetics

CMR distribution of hypertrophy, edema, fat, and late enhancement narrows mechanism. Genetic variants require phenotype, segregation, and careful classification; a VUS is not a diagnosis.

Electrical disease can lead

Conduction disease or ventricular arrhythmia may precede overt dysfunction in lamin, desmosomal, inflammatory, and infiltrative disease. Rhythm surveillance belongs in phenotyping.

II

Clinical reasoning

Build a cause matrix

Use age, tempo, pregnancy, toxins, infection, autoimmunity, neuromuscular signs, neuropathy, proteinuria, carpal tunnel, family history, and ancestry to target testing.

Screen relatives systematically

A three-generation pedigree plus ECG/imaging surveillance may be needed even when genetic testing is negative or indeterminate.

Treat mechanism and risk

Apply HF therapy where appropriate, remove toxins/triggers, treat infiltration/inflammation selectively, and assess ICD, obstruction, anticoagulation, exercise, and transplant needs.

Worked clinical problem

A 45-year-old with LV hypertrophy has low ECG voltage, carpal tunnel syndrome, proteinuria, and neuropathy.

1

Discordance

Thick walls with low voltage suggest infiltration rather than uncomplicated hypertensive hypertrophy.

2

Targeted workup

Evaluate monoclonal protein urgently and use bone scintigraphy/CMR according to the amyloid pathway.

3

Why urgent?

AL amyloidosis requires rapid hematologic treatment; ATTR has a different diagnostic and therapeutic route.

Synthesis

Pattern recognition should open a causal pathway, not stop at “LVH.” Extracardiac clues identify a potentially treatable infiltrative cardiomyopathy.

III

Retrieval practice

01Is “dilated cardiomyopathy” a complete etiology? +

No; it is a phenotype requiring a causal evaluation.

02What does a VUS prove? +

Nothing by itself; it should not drive predictive testing or major management as a pathogenic variant would.

03Why obtain a pedigree? +

Inheritance pattern and sudden-death or heart-failure clustering direct genetic evaluation and family screening.

Subspecialty chapters

Do not stop at the survey label

Dilated phenotype+

Mechanism

LV or biventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction can reflect genetic disease, myocarditis, toxins, tachycardia, pregnancy, metabolic disease, or ischemia.

Decision architecture

Exclude loading/ischemic causes, obtain pedigree and targeted CMR/labs/genetics, remove triggers, institute HF therapy, and assess arrhythmic and family risk.

Clinical anchor: “Dilated cardiomyopathy” is a phenotype, not the end of the causal workup.

Hypertrophic phenotype+

Mechanism

Sarcomeric HCM produces myocyte disarray, hypertrophy, fibrosis, diastolic dysfunction, ischemia, MR, and sometimes dynamic obstruction; phenocopies demand different treatment.

Decision architecture

Define distribution, obstruction at rest/provocation, symptoms, rhythm, scar, sudden-death markers, genotype/family implications, and candidacy for myosin inhibition or septal reduction.

Clinical anchor: Wall thickness alone cannot distinguish sarcomeric HCM from hypertension, athlete remodeling, storage, or infiltration.

Restrictive and infiltrative disease+

Mechanism

Stiff ventricles cause high filling pressure with relatively preserved chamber size; amyloid, sarcoid, iron, storage disease, fibrosis, and endomyocardial disorders are important causes.

Decision architecture

Use extracardiac clues, monoclonal protein testing, bone scintigraphy, CMR/PET, genetics, and selective biopsy to reach a treatment-specific diagnosis.

Clinical anchor: Thick walls plus low voltage or multisystem clues should trigger an infiltrative pathway.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy+

Mechanism

Desmosomal and other genetic disease replaces myocardium with scar/fat and creates ventricular arrhythmia, sometimes before overt dysfunction.

Decision architecture

Integrate ECG, ectopy morphology/burden, imaging, family history, genetics, exercise exposure, and tissue criteria; prescribe exercise restriction and sudden-death prevention deliberately.

Clinical anchor: Electrical disease can precede a structural phenotype.

Stress, inflammatory, and peripartum phenotypes+

Mechanism

Acute triggers can produce transient or persistent myocardial dysfunction through catecholamine toxicity, inflammation, autoimmunity, vascular dysfunction, or pregnancy-associated susceptibility.

Decision architecture

Exclude coronary occlusion and mechanical disease, define recovery with serial imaging, manage HF/rhythm risk, and tailor pregnancy, lactation, contraception, and recurrence counseling.

Clinical anchor: Apparent recovery of EF does not always erase recurrence or arrhythmic risk.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

Cardiomyopathy is myocardial disease not explained solely by loading conditions or coronary disease. Hypertrophic, dilated, arrhythmogenic, restrictive, and nondilated phenotypes are starting points; genetic, infiltrative, inflammatory, toxic, metabolic, and tachycardia-mediated causes cross those boundaries.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

Build a three-generation pedigree and look for sudden death, heart failure, transplant, devices, neuromuscular disease, and unexplained accidents. Search extracardiac clues: neuropathy, carpal tunnel, proteinuria, inflammation, skeletal weakness, hearing loss, and syndromic features.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Echo defines phenotype; CMR adds tissue characterization; selected laboratory, nuclear, genetic, and biopsy testing define cause. Genetic counseling should accompany testing because results reshape family screening.

→
4

Act and reassess

Treat the phenotype and the cause: heart failure, arrhythmia, obstruction, thrombosis, and sudden-death risk. Use disease-specific therapies only after adequate diagnostic confirmation.
