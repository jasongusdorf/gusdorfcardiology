Atrial fibrillation

Stabilize, prevent thromboembolism, control symptoms, and modify the substrate.

Mechanism

AF combines triggers with an atrial substrate shaped by age, pressure, obesity, sleep apnea, alcohol, inflammation, and structural disease. Electrical and structural remodeling make AF progressively easier to sustain.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Confirm AF and identify instability or reversible triggers.
  2. 02Estimate thromboembolic and bleeding risk.
  3. 03Choose rate, rhythm, anticoagulation, and substrate strategies.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Electrical substrate

Triggers—often pulmonary venous—interact with atrial dilation, fibrosis, inflammation, autonomic tone, and reentry. AF then promotes further electrical and structural remodeling.

Hemodynamic effect

Loss of atrial contraction and irregular rapid ventricular filling particularly impair stiff ventricles and mitral stenosis. Tachycardia can cause or worsen cardiomyopathy.

Thromboembolism

Blood stasis, endothelial dysfunction, and hypercoagulability favor left-atrial appendage thrombus. Stroke risk persists despite intermittent sinus rhythm because substrate and episodes may be silent.

II

Clinical reasoning

Four simultaneous jobs

Stabilize; prevent stroke; control rate and/or restore rhythm for symptoms and ventricular function; modify drivers such as obesity, sleep apnea, alcohol, hypertension, and inactivity.

Choose rhythm strategy deliberately

Symptoms, duration, atrial size, heart failure, early disease, patient preference, drug safety, and ablation candidacy matter. Cardioversion without maintenance planning is incomplete.

Anticoagulate by risk

Use a validated stroke-risk framework and contraindications; do not substitute aspirin. Plan around cardioversion, ablation, renal function, interactions, and adherence.

Worked clinical problem

A 58-year-old with new AF at 150/min, pulmonary edema, and hypotension arrives after two days of palpitations.

1

Stability

AF is contributing to shock and pulmonary edema.

2

Immediate action

Perform synchronized cardioversion; instability overrides delay for rate-control trials.

3

Thromboembolism plan

Because duration may exceed 48 hours, address pericardioversion anticoagulation and imaging strategy without delaying lifesaving cardioversion.

Synthesis

The sequence matters: stabilize first, then prevent stroke and recurrence while treating the substrate.

III

Retrieval practice

01Does sinus rhythm eliminate anticoagulation need? +

No; long-term therapy follows thromboembolic risk, not a spot rhythm.

02Why is AF poorly tolerated in mitral stenosis? +

Tachycardia shortens diastole and loss of atrial contraction worsens filling across an obstructed valve.

03What is the immediate therapy for unstable AF? +

Synchronized cardioversion.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

AF combines triggers with an atrial substrate shaped by age, pressure, obesity, sleep apnea, alcohol, inflammation, and structural disease. Electrical and structural remodeling make AF progressively easier to sustain.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

Establish AF on an ECG-quality tracing and define first-diagnosed, paroxysmal, persistent, or permanent status. Assess hemodynamic impact, symptom-rhythm correlation, duration, reversible drivers, structural disease, and stroke/bleeding risk.

→
3

Confirm and classify

ECG, electrolytes, CBC, renal/hepatic function, TSH, and echocardiography are common core tests. Use ambulatory monitoring when burden or symptom correlation will change therapy.

→
4

Act and reassess

Unstable AF requires synchronized cardioversion. Use validated stroke-risk assessment and anticoagulate when net benefit warrants; DOACs are preferred for most eligible patients. Choose rate versus rhythm control deliberately; early rhythm control and catheter ablation have larger roles than in older stepwise models. Treat obesity, inactivity, hypertension, sleep apnea, alcohol excess, and diabetes.
