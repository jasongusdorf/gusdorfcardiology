Pulmonary hypertension and right-heart failure

Classify the hemodynamic problem before applying disease-specific therapy.

Mechanism

Pulmonary hypertension arises from pulmonary vascular disease, left-heart disease, lung/hypoxic disease, chronic thromboembolism, or mixed/unclear mechanisms. The RV initially adapts by hypertrophy, then dilates as afterload exceeds contractile reserve.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Classify pulmonary hypertension by mechanism.
  • •Validate wedge, flow, and PVR on right-heart catheterization.
  • •Manage RV failure while avoiding harmful left-heart assumptions.
I

Anatomy and physiology

The RV is load-sensitive

The thin-walled RV tolerates volume but poorly tolerates acute afterload. Rising PVR increases wall stress, oxygen demand, dilation, TR, septal shift, and eventually reduces LV filling and systemic output.

Five mechanistic groups

Pulmonary arterial, left-heart, lung/hypoxic, chronic thromboembolic, and multifactorial PH require distinct evaluation and therapy. Echo estimates probability; catheterization defines hemodynamics.

Pre- versus postcapillary

Mean PA pressure, PA wedge pressure, cardiac output, and PVR distinguish patterns. Measurements must be technically valid and interpreted with volume, ventilation, and mitral disease.

II

Clinical reasoning

Search cause before vasodilator

History, V/Q imaging, pulmonary testing, sleep evaluation, CT, serologies, liver/portal assessment, and left-heart evaluation prevent mislabeling group 2/3/4 disease as PAH.

Protect RV perfusion

Avoid systemic hypotension, severe hypoxemia, acidosis, tachyarrhythmia, and indiscriminate fluid. Decongest venous overload while maintaining preload sufficient for output.

Use expert pathways

PAH combination therapy and CTEPH intervention require risk stratification and specialty expertise; pulmonary vasodilators may harm some left-heart or lung-disease phenotypes.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with severe RV dilation and shock receives repeated fluid boluses because the IVC is plethoric.

1

Misread physiology

A plethoric IVC and high JVP indicate high right-sided pressure, not automatic fluid responsiveness.

2

Mechanism

Additional volume worsens RV dilation, TR, wall stress, and septal shift, further reducing LV filling.

3

Action

Define cause, restore systemic pressure, reduce reversible PVR drivers, and decongest or support the RV according to hemodynamics.

Synthesis

The failing RV can be both preload-dependent and volume-overloaded. Therapy must target optimal—not maximal—preload while reducing afterload and preserving coronary perfusion.

Septal shift and pericardial constraint impair LV filling.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Pulmonary vascular resistance

PVR=COmPAP−PAWP​

Separates the transpulmonary gradient from the flow traversing it.

Units/terms: Wood units

Worked example

Given: mPAP 35; wedge 15 mmHg; CO 4 L/min

Substitute: (35 − 15) / 4

Result: 5 Wood units

Decision: Validate wedge and output before classifying pulmonary hypertension.

Transpulmonary gradient

TPG=mPAP−PAWP

A pressure difference, not a resistance; it remains flow-dependent.

Worked example

Given: mPAP 32; wedge 20 mmHg

Substitute: 32 − 20

Result: 12 mmHg

Decision: This is a flow-dependent pressure difference; PVR additionally divides by flow.

01

Findings

  • Look for loud P2, RV heave, TR, elevated JVP, edema, exertional presyncope, and low-output features.
  • Echo estimates probability and consequence; it does not establish hemodynamic class.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Right-heart catheterization confirms mean pulmonary artery pressure, wedge pressure, output, and PVR.
  • Complete etiologic workup before labeling idiopathic PAH; V/Q scanning remains central for CTEPH screening.
03

Management

  • Treat the underlying group; PAH-specific drugs can harm patients with left-heart or some lung-disease phenotypes.
  • In decompensated RV failure, preserve systemic pressure, avoid abrupt preload loss, correct hypoxia/acidosis, and involve a PH center early.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Treating an echo-estimated RVSP as a diagnosis.
  • Misclassifying wedge pressure from a poor tracing.
  • Giving large empiric fluid boluses to a failing, congested RV.
