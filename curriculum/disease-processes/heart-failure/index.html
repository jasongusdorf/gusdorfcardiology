Disease processes • Core

Heart failure

Define the syndrome, phenotype the ventricle, find the cause, and treat congestion and long-term risk in parallel.

Mechanism

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome caused by structural or functional cardiac abnormality with elevated filling pressures and/or inadequate output. EF phenotypes guide evidence-based therapy but do not replace etiologic diagnosis. Neurohormonal activation preserves perfusion short-term while driving sodium retention, vasoconstriction, and remodeling.

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

Heart failure means the pump cannot fill or eject without harmful pressure or inadequate output. Emergency hormones help briefly but later retain salt, tighten vessels, and remodel the heart.

↔

A mental model that sticks

A weak pump raises tank level and squeezes pipes to compensate—useful for minutes, harmful for months.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Explain four layers: congestion/perfusion, EF phenotype, cause, and precipitant; then separate stabilization from survival therapy.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. EF is not the syndrome.
  • 2. Name four HFrEF pillars.
  • 3. Keep searching for cause.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Define syndrome, phenotype, cause, stage, and precipitant separately.
  2. 02Treat acute congestion/perfusion while protecting long-term outcomes.
  3. 03Initiate and sequence disease-modifying therapy.
I

Anatomy and physiology

A syndrome, not an EF

Heart failure is symptoms/signs from structural or functional cardiac abnormality corroborated by congestion, biomarkers, or imaging. EF classifies phenotype but does not measure filling pressure or exclude low output.

Compensation becomes disease

RAAS, sympathetic activation, vasopressin, and remodeling preserve perfusion acutely but drive sodium retention, vasoconstriction, arrhythmia, and fibrosis chronically.

Find the cause

Ischemia, hypertension, valve disease, toxins, tachycardia, inflammation, infiltration, genetics, high output, and pericardial disease require different interventions.

II

Clinical reasoning

Acute first hour

Assess oxygenation, congestion, perfusion, pressure, ischemia, arrhythmia, and mechanical catastrophe. Use diuretic, vasodilator, inotrope, or support according to phenotype—not by habit.

Build foundational therapy

For HFrEF, combine ARNI/RAAS inhibition, evidence-based beta blockade, mineralocorticoid antagonism, and SGLT2 inhibition as tolerated, then add phenotype-specific therapy.

Transitions are treatment

Resolve precipitant, achieve an oral diuretic plan, reconcile medications, teach monitoring, arrange early follow-up, and address iron, rhythm, valve, device, and palliative needs.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with EF 25%, pulmonary edema, BP 180/100, and warm extremities arrives in severe distress.

1

Phenotype

Wet-warm hypertensive acute heart failure with high afterload, not primary low-output shock.

2

Immediate physiology

Noninvasive ventilation, IV loop diuretic, and rapid vasodilation can reduce filling pressure and impedance.

3

After stabilization

Identify precipitant and re-establish all tolerated disease-modifying pillars before discharge.

Synthesis

The high blood pressure is part of the mechanism. Treating afterload and congestion is more rational than reflex inotropy.

III

Retrieval practice

01Does normal EF exclude HF? +

No. HFpEF can produce high filling pressure and congestion.

02Name the four foundational HFrEF drug classes. +

ARNI/ACEi/ARB, evidence-based beta blocker, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor.

03What are the two bedside axes? +

Congestion and perfusion.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

EF phenotypes guide evidence-based therapy but do not replace etiologic diagnosis. Neurohormonal activation preserves perfusion short-term while driving sodium retention, vasoconstriction, and remodeling.

2

Recognize the pattern

Confirm congestion and/or hypoperfusion; identify precipitants such as ischemia, arrhythmia, infection, medication changes, and uncontrolled pressure. Separate HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFimpEF, and HFpEF while evaluating ischemic, valvular, infiltrative, toxic, inflammatory, genetic, and tachycardia-mediated causes.

→
3

Confirm and classify

ECG, natriuretic peptide, renal/hepatic function, iron studies, TSH, chest imaging, and echocardiography form the usual first pass. Use ischemic evaluation, CMR, genetics, or invasive hemodynamics when the phenotype or trajectory warrants.

→
4

Act and reassess

Relieve congestion and stabilize perfusion first. For chronic HFrEF, establish the four foundational drug classes early at tolerated doses; address devices, iron deficiency, rehabilitation, and advanced-therapy triggers. For HFpEF, use SGLT2 inhibition, careful volume control, and aggressive treatment of the causal comorbidity cluster.

Common pitfalls

  • Withholding all disease-modifying therapy for asymptomatic low blood pressure.
  • Calling every edema/dyspnea presentation HF without objective cardiac evidence.
  • Failing to revisit etiology after the EF label is assigned.
