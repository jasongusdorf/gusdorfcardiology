Echocardiography and cardiac POCUS

Use ultrasound to answer a focused hemodynamic question without outrunning image quality.

Mechanism

Two-dimensional imaging shows anatomy and motion; Doppler converts frequency shift into velocity; gradients are derived from velocity; chamber pressures are inferred through validated relationships with important assumptions.

Visual atlas

See the structure, then explain the physiology

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

Ultrasound sends sound into the chest and listens for echoes. It shows anatomy, motion, and velocity, but every answer depends on view and assumptions.

↔

A mental model that sticks

It is sonar through a narrow window: a poor angle can make normal structures look short and jets look slow.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Choose one focused question and state required views, image limitations, decisive finding, and when comprehensive imaging is needed.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Name the view.
  • 2. Explain Doppler angle.
  • 3. Do not outrun image quality.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Acquire and orient standard views.
  2. 02Explain Doppler assumptions and common quantitative errors.
  3. 03Define what focused ultrasound can and cannot answer.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Views are anatomic slices

Parasternal, apical, subcostal, and suprasternal windows intersect the same three-dimensional heart. Foreshortening, off-axis cuts, and gain can fabricate size or function abnormalities.

Doppler measures velocity

Continuous-wave Doppler captures high velocity without depth specificity; pulsed-wave Doppler localizes at the cost of aliasing; color Doppler maps direction and velocity variance. Alignment error underestimates velocity.

Pressure is derived

The simplified Bernoulli equation converts velocity to gradient. Valve area, pulmonary pressure, filling pressure, and output rely on measurements and assumptions that must be checked for internal consistency.

II

Clinical reasoning

Begin with a question

“Is there tamponade physiology?” is answerable; “tell me everything” is not. Document views, quality, findings, and limits.

Integrate rather than isolate

RV size, IVC, B-lines, valve jets, and EF are context-dependent. Mechanical ventilation, TR, pulmonary hypertension, and abdominal pressure can invalidate shortcuts.

Escalate when precision changes care

Formal TTE, TEE, CT, or CMR is needed for valve quantification, endocarditis, congenital anatomy, tissue characterization, or intervention planning.

Worked clinical problem

POCUS in a ventilated patient shows a plethoric IVC and a hyperdynamic, small LV after hypotension develops.

1

Avoid shortcut

A plethoric IVC on positive-pressure ventilation does not establish volume overload.

2

Physiology

The small hyperdynamic LV may reflect low preload, vasodilation, or obstructive physiology.

3

Next step

Integrate RV findings, lung ultrasound, pressure, passive-leg-raise response, and the clinical event before fluid or diuretic therapy.

Synthesis

One ultrasound sign is not a volume diagnosis. The value of POCUS is rapid physiologic integration with explicit limits.

III

Retrieval practice

01Why does Doppler misalignment underestimate gradients? +

Measured velocity is the true velocity multiplied by the cosine of the intercept angle.

02Does an effusion equal tamponade? +

No; tamponade is hemodynamic compromise from pericardial pressure.

03Why is apical foreshortening harmful? +

It truncates the LV and distorts volumes, EF, and wall-motion assessment.

A reusable reasoning framework

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Continuity equation

A1​v1​=A2​v2​

Conservation of flow allows valve area to be derived from upstream area and velocity-time integrals.

Worked example

Given: LVOT area 3.1 cm²; LVOT VTI 20 cm; aortic VTI 80 cm

Substitute: 3.1 × 20 / 80

Result: AVA ≈ 0.78 cm²

Decision: Recheck LVOT diameter and Doppler alignment before labeling discordant severe AS.

LV mass (ASE)

LVM=0.8{1.04[(IVS+LVID+PWT)3−LVID3]}+0.6

A geometric estimate that is highly sensitive to measurement error because dimensions are cubed.

Units/terms: Linear dimensions in cm; mass in g

Worked example

Given: IVS 1.1 cm; LVID 5.0 cm; PWT 1.1 cm

Substitute: 0.8{1.04[(7.2)³ − 5.0³]} + 0.6

Result: ≈207 g

Decision: Index to body size and remember that cubed dimensions amplify small measurement errors.
