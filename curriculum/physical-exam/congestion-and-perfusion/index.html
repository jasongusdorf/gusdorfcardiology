Physical exam • Core

Congestion and perfusion at the bedside

Place the patient on two axes: wet versus dry and warm versus cold.

Mechanism

Filling pressure and forward flow are related but not interchangeable. A patient may be markedly congested with preserved perfusion or have low output without dramatic edema. Venous congestion itself can worsen kidney and liver function.

0

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

“Wet” asks whether pressure backs fluid up; “cold” asks whether enough blood moves forward. Either problem can occur without the other.

↔

A mental model that sticks

A flooded basement and weak water pressure are different plumbing failures.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Place four patients into wet/dry and warm/cold quadrants, give bedside evidence, and name the first objective for each.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Use JVP for congestion.
  • 2. Use skin, brain, urine, and pulse for perfusion.
  • 3. Explain creatinine during decongestion.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

  • •Assess filling pressure and forward perfusion independently.
  • •Integrate examination, ultrasound, laboratory trends, and response to therapy.
  • •Avoid reflex fluid or premature cessation of decongestion.
I

Anatomy and physiology

Two axes

Congestion reflects elevated filling pressures and sodium/water retention; hypoperfusion reflects inadequate effective flow. Wet/dry and warm/cold produce four clinically different profiles.

Venous organ injury

High renal venous pressure lowers the filtration gradient; hepatic congestion produces cholestatic or hepatocellular injury; bowel edema can impair absorption. Congestion itself is pathogenic.

Compensated pressure

SVR can preserve blood pressure despite low cardiac output. Warm skin and mentation do not guarantee normal filling pressure, and edema does not establish intravascular volume.

II

Clinical reasoning

Triangulate congestion

JVP, orthopnea, bendopnea, edema, lung B-lines, venous Doppler, weight, and natriuretic peptides each sample different consequences. Discordance should trigger reassessment.

Interpret creatinine dynamically

A modest creatinine rise during effective decongestion may reflect hemodynamic change rather than tubular injury. Persistent congestion often carries greater risk.

Reassess after each intervention

Track symptoms, JVP, urine sodium/output, pressure, perfusion, electrolytes, and renal function rather than treating a static target weight.

Worked clinical problem

A patient with HFrEF remains orthopneic with JVP 14 cm after diuresis; creatinine rises from 1.2 to 1.5 mg/dL, blood pressure and perfusion remain stable.

1

Dominant problem

Objective congestion persists.

2

Renal interpretation

The modest creatinine change does not by itself prove harmful volume depletion.

3

Action

Continue carefully monitored decongestion, assess natriuretic response and diuretic delivery, and reassess perfusion.

Synthesis

A laboratory change should not override persistent bedside physiology. The goal is effective decongestion without hypoperfusion, not preservation of a single creatinine value.

III

Retrieval practice

01Can a patient be wet and warm? +

Yes; this is common in decompensated heart failure.

02Why can congestion worsen kidney function? +

Raised renal venous pressure reduces the effective filtration gradient.

03Does peripheral edema prove high cardiac filling pressure? +

No; local venous, lymphatic, medication, and oncotic causes can produce edema.

Equations that sharpen the bedside model

Pulse pressure

PP=SBP−DBP

A narrow pulse pressure can be a bedside clue to low stroke volume, but arterial compliance and measurement conditions matter.

Worked example

Given: BP 88/72 mmHg

Substitute: 88 − 72

Result: 16 mmHg

Decision: A narrow pulse pressure supports low stroke volume only in the appropriate compliance and measurement context.

01

Findings

  • Congestion: elevated JVP, edema, ascites, orthopnea, bendopnea, rales, and rapid weight gain.
  • Hypoperfusion: narrow pulse pressure, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, altered mentation, oliguria, and rising lactate.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Use lung and venous ultrasound when the examination is uncertain, but integrate them with the clinical context.
  • Trend urine output, renal function, lactate, and blood pressure response.
03

Management

  • Wet-warm patients generally need decongestion; cold profiles require urgent definition of cause and hemodynamics.
  • Do not stop effective decongestion solely for a modest creatinine rise if congestion is improving and perfusion is preserved.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Using peripheral edema alone to diagnose intravascular overload.
  • Giving reflex fluids for creatinine elevation despite clear venous congestion.
