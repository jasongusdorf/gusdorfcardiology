Physical exam • Core

Congestion and perfusion at the bedside

Place the patient on two axes: wet versus dry and warm versus cold.

Mechanism

Filling pressure and forward flow are related but not interchangeable. A patient may be markedly congested with preserved perfusion or have low output without dramatic edema. Venous congestion itself can worsen kidney and liver function.

Build intuition first

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

“Wet” asks whether pressure backs fluid up; “cold” asks whether enough blood moves forward. Either problem can occur without the other.

↔

A mental model that sticks

A flooded basement and weak water pressure are different plumbing failures.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Place four patients into wet/dry and warm/cold quadrants, give bedside evidence, and name the first objective for each.

Your explanation is complete only if it can answer:

  • 1. Use JVP for congestion.
  • 2. Use skin, brain, urine, and pulse for perfusion.
  • 3. Explain creatinine during decongestion.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

Filling pressure and forward flow are related but not interchangeable. A patient may be markedly congested with preserved perfusion or have low output without dramatic edema. Venous congestion itself can worsen kidney and liver function.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

Congestion: elevated JVP, edema, ascites, orthopnea, bendopnea, rales, and rapid weight gain. Hypoperfusion: narrow pulse pressure, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, altered mentation, oliguria, and rising lactate.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Use lung and venous ultrasound when the examination is uncertain, but integrate them with the clinical context. Trend urine output, renal function, lactate, and blood pressure response.

→
4

Act and reassess

Wet-warm patients generally need decongestion; cold profiles require urgent definition of cause and hemodynamics. Do not stop effective decongestion solely for a modest creatinine rise if congestion is improving and perfusion is preserved.

By the end, you should be able to

  • 01Explain the underlying mechanism without relying on a memorized label.
  • 02Recognize the highest-yield clinical and physiologic findings.
  • 03Choose a test because of the decision it can change.
  • 04Start appropriate management and name what must be reassessed.

Check your understanding

  1. Mechanism: What variable changes first, and what changes downstream?
  2. Discrimination: Which finding best separates this process from its closest mimic?
  3. Testing: What result would actually change what you do next?
  4. Safety: Which common shortcut creates the greatest risk? Hint: Using peripheral edema alone to diagnose intravascular overload.
01

Findings

  • Congestion: elevated JVP, edema, ascites, orthopnea, bendopnea, rales, and rapid weight gain.
  • Hypoperfusion: narrow pulse pressure, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, altered mentation, oliguria, and rising lactate.
02

Tests and interpretation

  • Use lung and venous ultrasound when the examination is uncertain, but integrate them with the clinical context.
  • Trend urine output, renal function, lactate, and blood pressure response.
03

Management

  • Wet-warm patients generally need decongestion; cold profiles require urgent definition of cause and hemodynamics.
  • Do not stop effective decongestion solely for a modest creatinine rise if congestion is improving and perfusion is preserved.
04

Common pitfalls

  • Using peripheral edema alone to diagnose intravascular overload.
  • Giving reflex fluids for creatinine elevation despite clear venous congestion.
