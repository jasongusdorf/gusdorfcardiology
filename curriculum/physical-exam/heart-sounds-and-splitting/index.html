Physical exam • Core

Heart sounds and splitting

Translate S1–S4, splitting, clicks, snaps, and rubs into valve motion and filling physiology.

Mechanism

S1 and S2 mark closure of the AV and semilunar valves. Respiratory splitting of S2 follows delayed pulmonic closure during inspiration. Extra sounds occur when flow abruptly decelerates, a valve reaches its opening limit, or inflamed pericardial surfaces move against one another.

Three layers of understanding

Start simple, add a mental model, then prove you understand it by teaching the mechanism back without jargon.

5

Explain it like I’m five

Heart sounds occur when moving blood and cardiac structures abruptly stop or change direction. Timing identifies the valves and chambers involved.

A mental model that sticks

A washer makes clicks at cycle changes and extra thumps when water rushes into a stiff or overfilled drum.

The analogy is a scaffold—not a substitute for the physiology.

Feynman technique

Teach it back

Tap out S1 and S2, mark systole and diastole, then place S3, S4, an opening snap, and a click while explaining what physically creates each.

  • 2. Explain respiratory S2 splitting.
  • 3. Distinguish S3 from an opening snap.

Masterclass

From structure to bedside decisions

This is the working layer: build the mechanism, discriminate among competing explanations, and use the result to make a decision.

Learning objectives

  1. 01Time sounds within the cardiac cycle before naming them.
  2. 02Explain S2 splitting from ventricular ejection and conduction.
  3. 03Distinguish extra sounds by timing, frequency, location, and maneuvers.
I

Anatomy and physiology

S1 and S2

S1 follows AV-valve closure and is influenced by leaflet position, PR interval, and contractility. S2 contains aortic and pulmonic components; intensity and separation reflect closure pressure and ejection timing.

Diastolic sounds

S3 occurs during rapid early filling and may reflect volume loading or normal youthful compliance. S4 follows atrial contraction into a noncompliant ventricle and is absent in atrial fibrillation.

Clicks, snaps, and rubs

An ejection click follows semilunar opening; a midsystolic click reflects abrupt mitral apparatus tension; an opening snap follows mitral opening; a rub may have up to three components and varies over time.

II

Clinical reasoning

Anchor to the carotid

The sound just before the carotid upstroke is S1; the sound after the pulse is S2. Then place extra sounds by interval and frequency.

Explain splitting

Inspiration increases RV filling and prolongs RV ejection while reducing LV filling, delaying P2 relative to A2. Fixed, wide, or paradoxical patterns narrow the mechanism.

Use sound to choose imaging

Auscultation generates a structural hypothesis and urgency grade; echocardiography defines anatomy and severity.

Worked clinical problem

A patient has exertional dyspnea and an S2 that remains widely split during both inspiration and expiration.

1

Observation

The split is fixed rather than normally respiratory.

2

Mechanism

Atrial-level shunting chronically increases RV stroke volume and delays pulmonic closure with little respiratory variation.

3

Next test

Echocardiography should assess right-heart size, septal anatomy, and shunt.

Synthesis

Fixed splitting is a hemodynamic sign, not a diagnosis; the next step is to demonstrate the responsible shunt and its consequences.

III

Retrieval practice

01Why is S4 absent in AF? +

There is no coordinated atrial contraction.

02What causes normal inspiratory splitting? +

Later P2 from increased RV filling/ejection plus slightly earlier A2 from reduced LV filling.

03Which is higher frequency: S3 or an opening snap? +

An opening snap.

Deep dive

A reusable reasoning framework

Move from physiology to pattern recognition, diagnostic proof, and an action that changes the patient’s trajectory.

1

Build the mechanism

S1 and S2 mark closure of the AV and semilunar valves. Respiratory splitting of S2 follows delayed pulmonic closure during inspiration. Extra sounds occur when flow abruptly decelerates, a valve reaches its opening limit, or inflamed pericardial surfaces move against one another.

→
2

Recognize the pattern

Fixed S2 splitting suggests an atrial-level shunt; paradoxical splitting reflects delayed LV emptying. An opening snap after S2 suggests a mobile stenotic mitral valve; a systolic click that moves with loading conditions suggests mitral valve prolapse.

→
3

Confirm and classify

Listen at multiple sites with diaphragm and bell, then repeat in left lateral and upright positions. Time sounds against the carotid pulse when systole is uncertain.

→
4

Act and reassess

Use the sound to choose confirmatory imaging rather than treating auscultation as a final severity grade.
