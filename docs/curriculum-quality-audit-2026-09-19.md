# Gusdorf Cardiology Curriculum Quality Audit

**Date:** September 19, 2026  
**Scope:** Six curriculum tracks, 28 lessons  
**Method:** Fixed 100-point rubric, source inspection, production-build inspection, and automated inventory of diagrams, figures, formulas, guideline mappings, external resources, worked cases, and retrieval questions.

## Executive judgment

**Overall grade: 78/100 (B+, approximately 7.8/10).**

The rebuild moved the curriculum well beyond its prior outline form. Every lesson now has a coherent mechanism-first teaching sequence, specific objectives, a worked clinical problem, synthesis, and retrieval practice. Foundations is close to an excellent open educational resource. The site is not yet 10/10 across tracks because visual density falls sharply after Foundations, several large fields are compressed into single survey lessons, equations are usually interpreted but rarely worked numerically, assessment is formative rather than mastery-based, and clinical claims still lack granular inline citations and named physician peer review.

## Rubric

| Domain | Weight | Full-credit standard |
|---|---:|---|
| Accuracy and evidence | 20 | Current guideline concordance; precise claims; primary/authoritative sources attached to claims; uncertainty stated; dated review; named clinical peer review. |
| Depth and completeness | 15 | Appropriate scope from anatomy/pathophysiology through diagnosis, severity, acute care, longitudinal care, complications, and special populations; no major topic compressed beyond usefulness. |
| Pedagogy and organization | 15 | Explicit objectives and prerequisites; layered explanation; mechanism before memorization; coherent progression; signaling, chunking, and summaries; cognitive load managed. |
| Clinical reasoning and application | 15 | Illness scripts, discriminators, mimics, severity, decision thresholds, worked cases, reassessment, and common failure modes; recommendations connected to patient context. |
| Visual and multimedia learning | 10 | Multiple purposeful, legible, accessible visuals where the topic is spatial, temporal, or pattern-based; media embedded near the concept; captions teach rather than decorate; licensing/attribution complete. |
| Quantitative reasoning | 10 | Relevant equations with units, assumptions, limitations, and at least one worked numerical example; connects result to a clinical decision; avoids formula wallpaper. |
| Assessment and mastery | 10 | Retrieval plus application questions, answer explanations, escalating difficulty, feedback, coverage blueprint, and a way to demonstrate mastery or revisit weak areas. |
| Usability and accessibility | 5 | Responsive navigation, readable typography, keyboard/semantic structure, descriptive alt text, transcripts for audio/video, good performance, and clear lesson sequencing. |

### Grade bands

- **90–100 / A:** publication-grade, comprehensive, visually rich, evidence-traceable, mastery-assessed, and peer-reviewed.
- **80–89 / B:** strong teaching resource with clear educational value; important gaps remain.
- **70–79 / C:** useful but uneven; some domains depend on summaries or links rather than complete teaching.
- **60–69 / D:** broad outline with limited depth, application, or verification.
- **Below 60:** inadequate as a stand-alone curriculum.

## Section findings

### 1. Foundations — 88/100

**What is strong**

- The best visual coverage in the curriculum: eight sourced figures plus six custom diagrams across four lessons.
- Cardiac anatomy now combines a gross specimen, anterior surface view, internal cutaway, coronary territories, conduction map, and circulation diagram.
- All four lessons have equations, units/interpretation, a masterclass, worked case, retrieval questions, and layered intuitive explanations.
- Mechanism is consistently translated into examination, imaging, ECG, hemodynamic, or management consequences.

**Deductions**

- Equations generally lack step-by-step numerical examples and sensitivity analysis.
- No explicit prerequisite map or cumulative mastery checkpoint before moving to later tracks.
- Sources are listed at lesson/track level rather than cited beside individual claims.
- The gross anatomy atlas could add posterior/inferior surfaces, valve-base orientation, pericardial relations, and cross-sectional imaging correlation.

**Priority to reach A:** add annotated CT/echo correlation, two worked calculations per quantitative lesson, inline references, and an end-of-track mastery assessment.

### 2. Physical exam — 82/100

**What is strong**

- Excellent mechanism-to-bedside translation in sounds, murmurs, JVP, congestion, and perfusion.
- Every lesson has a custom explanatory diagram or sourced figure; the heart-sound library supplies a major practical asset.
- Dynamic maneuvers and venous waveforms are taught causally rather than as memorized associations.
- Worked cases model the important error patterns: quiet acute MR, fixed splitting, TR waveforms, and creatinine during decongestion.

**Deductions**

- Audio is linked but not embedded at the exact teaching moment with synchronized phonocardiogram/ECG labels.
- No video demonstrations of patient positioning, JVP measurement, hepatojugular reflux, or dynamic maneuvers.
- Physical examination performance is not assessed with observation checklists or image/audio identification sets.
- Quantitative content is appropriate but thin; the heart-sounds lesson has no mathematical element, which is reasonable but lowers track-wide quantitative coverage.

**Priority to reach A:** embed representative sounds and bedside videos with transcripts, add timed identification drills, and create an examination-performance checklist.

### 3. Diagnostic interpretation — 84/100

**What is strong**

- Strong quantitative coverage: eight formulas across five lessons, including QT correction, continuity, LV mass, biomarker change, Fick, SVR, and PVR.
- High-quality error awareness: lead reversal, artifact, foreshortening, Doppler alignment, false wedge pressure, and biomarker misclassification.
- ECG library and imaging/hemodynamics lab create genuine deliberate-practice opportunities beyond prose.
- Each lesson explicitly names what the test can answer and when image or measurement quality invalidates the conclusion.

**Deductions**

- Telemetry/artifact has no embedded representative strips or figures within the lesson.
- Echo has only one sourced image despite being an intrinsically visual subject; standard windows need labeled stills and normal cine loops embedded in sequence.
- Hemodynamics needs labeled raw waveforms with normal values and side-by-side pathologic examples.
- No formal competency sets for ECG interpretation, Doppler calculation, biomarker classification, or catheter-tracing validation.

**Priority to reach A:** build embedded normal-to-abnormal visual sequences, add numerical calculation problems with answers, and create scored interpretation sets.

### 4. Disease processes — 74/100

**What is strong**

- Strong mechanism-first overviews for HF, valve disease, AF, ACS, cardiomyopathy, inflammatory disease, and pulmonary hypertension.
- Eleven guideline-topic mappings give this track the broadest guideline coverage.
- Cases emphasize dangerous distinctions such as type 1 versus type 2 MI, low-flow low-gradient AS, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, myocardial involvement, and RV volume loading.

**Deductions**

- Four of seven lessons have no embedded figure or custom diagram: ACS, cardiomyopathies, pericardial/myocardial inflammation, and pulmonary hypertension/right-heart failure.
- Three lessons have no equation; other disease lessons use only one or two equations without worked examples.
- “Valvular heart disease” and “cardiomyopathies” are too broad for single lessons. Each encompasses multiple diseases with different anatomy, severity metrics, and treatment thresholds.
- Major cardiology disease domains are absent as stand-alone lessons: hypertension, dyslipidemia/prevention, aortic disease, endocarditis, congenital heart disease, ventricular arrhythmias, brady/conduction disease, cardio-oncology, pregnancy, and vascular medicine.
- Inline evidence traceability and physician peer review are still pending.

**Priority to reach A:** split survey lessons into disease-specific modules, fill the missing disease domains, and build visual pathology/ECG/imaging atlases with current claim-level guideline citations.

### 5. Treatments and procedures — 73/100

**What is strong**

- Good conceptual separation of indication, mechanism, expected benefit, failure modes, monitoring, and reassessment.
- Procedure cases correctly emphasize physiology and safety: intermediate coronary lesions, inappropriate ICD shocks, and pre-excited AF.
- Guideline mappings are broad, and every lesson includes at least one quantitative concept.

**Deductions**

- All four lessons have zero embedded sourced figures or custom diagrams. This is the largest visual failure in the curriculum.
- Coronary anatomy/angiographic projections, stent mechanics, device lead positions, pacing timing cycles, electrograms, ablation circuits, and pad placement are taught without the images they require.
- Pharmacology is compressed into one mechanism survey without dosing/titration tables, adverse-effect comparisons, renal/hepatic adjustments, or common interaction cases.
- Procedure complications and post-procedure care are discussed but not taught through visual algorithms or branching simulations.

**Priority to reach A:** create procedural visual sequences and device tracings first; then split pharmacology by therapeutic domain and add practical dosing/monitoring tables.

### 6. Cases and practice — 74/100

**What is strong**

- Highest score for clinical application: every lesson centers risk, discriminators, time course, serial data, and reassessment.
- Worked problems are concise and model good sequencing rather than retrospective diagnosis.
- The separate Case Lab provides additional practice beyond the four symptom lessons.

**Deductions**

- Three of four lessons have no embedded visual. Chest pain needs serial ECG/aortic/PE examples; dyspnea needs radiography/ultrasound/hemodynamic integration; syncope needs ECG and rhythm-strip cases.
- Four presenting problems are not a complete cardiology case curriculum. Missing presentations include bradycardia, wide-complex tachycardia, hypertensive emergency, post-procedural complications, endocarditis, congenital disease, pregnancy, cardio-oncology, and perioperative consultation.
- Retrieval questions are unscored reveal cards. There is no confidence rating, adaptive repetition, performance tracking, or explanation of distractors.
- Most equations are presented conceptually rather than calculated from case data.

**Priority to reach A:** convert representative cases into branching, scored cases with serial ECG/imaging/laboratory data and feedback tied to specific errors.

## Highest-value roadmap

### Phase 1: fix the largest quality asymmetry

1. Add visual sequences to all 12 visually empty lessons.
2. Prioritize treatments/procedures, where all four lessons currently lack embedded visuals.
3. Use local, licensed media with captions that ask the learner to identify a structure, pattern, or decision—not decorative images.

### Phase 2: replace surveys with teachable units

1. Split valve disease by lesion and cardiomyopathy by phenotype/cause.
2. Add absent core disease modules: hypertension, prevention/lipids, aorta, endocarditis, congenital disease, ventricular arrhythmia, and conduction disease.
3. Expand procedure and pharmacology content into practical submodules.

### Phase 3: build real mastery assessment

1. Add numeric worked examples with units, assumptions, and sensitivity to measurement error.
2. Add scored normal-to-abnormal interpretation sets for ECG, echo, hemodynamics, auscultation, and devices.
3. Add branching cases, confidence ratings, spaced repetition, and weak-domain feedback.

### Phase 4: publication-grade evidence and review

1. Add claim-level citations to current ACC/AHA, ESC, ASE, HRS, SCAI, and primary evidence.
2. Record search/review dates and evidence limitations.
3. Complete named cardiologist peer review and document corrections.
4. Add an update schedule for guideline-sensitive lessons.

## Bottom line

The curriculum is now a **strong B+ overall**, not a 10/10. Foundations is the closest to excellent. Diagnostic interpretation is strong but under-illustrated. Physical examination needs embedded audiovisual teaching. Disease processes, treatments/procedures, and cases remain limited by visual scarcity, compressed scope, and non-mastery assessment. The most defensible route to 10/10 is not more prose; it is targeted visuals, smaller disease-specific modules, worked quantitative problems, scored deliberate practice, claim-level evidence, and clinical peer review.
